Island 1.1: round 5, from real infographics

A researcher studied how multi-part infographics are built (Cell Press graphical abstracts, Bang Wong in Nature Methods, four-panel comics, Hergé's pages, Isotype, safety cards…). What they agree on:

  1. Divide one field; do not scatter four objects. The good pages are one frame cut by gutters (comics, Cell's "one split panel", Kahn's rooms, 2.1's column). Four free-floating containers with arrows is the template look.
  2. The structure carries the sequence; decoration does not. Position, number, alignment and a regular grid say "next" (STAR Protocols, waffle-iron, safety cards). Surface effects were never what made these pages good.
  3. Separation is cheap. Use a thin rule or shared wall plus a constant gutter, or a faint tint. IKEA, Isotype, safety cards and 2.1 all separate with hairlines and tint.
  4. One deliberate departure from regularity. It goes on the turn (*ten*). The departure is in the cell's span or its tint, never in the landmark's scale.
  5. Colour carries meaning along the reading order. Cell, Wong and 2.1 all use English blue to translated lilac.
  6. The row return is a designed event. It can be a cue at the end of the row, or the gutter itself. It should not be a long diagonal across empty paper.
  7. Align everything to guides. "Small misalignments" are what read as amateur (Wong).

3

C · Numbered cards: four identical cards, a thin tinted header band with a numeral disc 1–4, the tint moving blue → lilac (STAR Protocols).

4

D · The carriage-return gutter: one continuous guide runs through the gutters like a typewriter line: along row 1, back left, along row 2.

5

E · The Isotype plate: no box per landmark; two rows on a shared ruled baseline, thin vertical rules, one hairline frame.
